# Reasoning principles

Plain rules of thumb that seats may cite. The Chair pastes this list into the round prompts.

How to cite one: put its short name in brackets after the argument, for example `(base rates)`.

A principle is a lens, not a proof. It explains why a seat weighs a fact the way it does. The facts
in the brief still decide. A seat may use a principle that is not on this list if it states it in
one plain sentence. It never attributes a principle to a person or a book.

## About evidence

1. **Base rates.** Before the story of why this case is special, ask how things like this usually
   turn out. Start from the usual result and move away from it only for a reason you can name.
2. **Actions over words.** What people did (paid, renewed, cancelled, came back) outweighs what
   they said they would do. Praise is cheap. A payment is evidence.
3. **Small samples.** Three happy customers are three stories, not a trend. Say how many cases a
   claim rests on before you lean on it.
4. **Incentives.** Ask who gains if a claim is believed. A number from someone paid to be hopeful
   needs a second source.
5. **Unknown is an answer.** "We do not know yet" is better than a confident guess. Mark the gap,
   and say what would close it.

## About the decision

6. **Reversibility.** Sort the decision first. If it is cheap to undo, decide fast and learn from
   the result. If it is hard to undo, slow down and test first. Watch for decisions that only look
   easy to undo.
7. **Survive first.** No bet whose loss ends the company, however good the average outcome looks.
   You have to stay in the game to collect on the good bets.
8. **Opportunity cost.** The real price of an option is the best other thing the same time and
   money could have bought. Name that other thing.
9. **Cost of delay.** Waiting is a choice with a price. Say what each month of delay costs, and
   what each month of waiting would teach.
10. **Sunk cost.** Money and time already spent are gone under every option. Only what happens from
    today counts.
11. **Second-order effects.** Ask "and then what?" at least twice. The first effect is usually the
    one that was planned. The second is the one that hurts.
12. **What must be true.** List the few things that have to hold for the option to work. Find the
    one that is least checked. That is where the risk sits.

## About doing it

13. **Smallest test.** Before the build, run the cheapest thing that could prove the riskiest
    assumption wrong. Write down what counts as a pass before you run it.
14. **Stop rule first.** Decide what result would make you stop before you spend, not after. A stop
    rule written later always moves.
15. **One bottleneck.** At any moment one thing limits growth more than the rest. Work on anything
    else and the result barely changes. Name the one.
16. **Narrow first.** Win one clear group of customers fully before reaching for the next. Half a
    win in four groups is harder to defend than a full win in one.
17. **Price against the alternative.** Buyers compare the price with what they would do instead,
    not with what it costs to make. Know that alternative before setting a price.
18. **A named person and a number.** A recurring task with nobody named for it and no number
    watched each week will quietly stop. If it matters, give it both.
19. **Smaller version.** Most ideas have a version that delivers most of the value for a fraction
    of the work. Put it on the table as an option every time.

## How seats misuse principles

- Citing one as if it settled the matter. It never does. A fact does.
- Picking the principle that fits a conclusion already reached. If two principles pull in opposite
  directions (cost of delay against smallest test, for example), say so. That tension is a crux.
- Hiding a guess behind a principle. A guessed number is still marked `(assumed)`.
